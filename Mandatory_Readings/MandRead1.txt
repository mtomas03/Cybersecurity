## Task 1: Summary of the Reading

In the article IoT is wildly Insecure and often unpatchable, security expert Schneier explains that the Internet of Things (IoT) has entered a severe crisis due to the widespread of unpatchable vulnerabilities inherent to embedded systems. He makes a historical parallel with the PC crisis of the mid-1990s, when PCs faced similar widespread insecurity while software vendors kept vulnerabilities secret and released patches very slowly. Back then, the problem was mitigated by the implementation of automatic updates and the full public disclosure of vulnerabilities, which forced companies to address flaws in a timely manner. Today, however, the situation is far more critical because an immensely large number of vulnerable embedded devices are easily reachable via the Internet, while their producers lack the capability to manage security and have little incentive to do so due to thin profit margins. This structural vulnerability becomes clear when analysing the market of these products, which are typically the result of assembling various software and hardware components from different companies with very poor engineering. Furthermore, these components are already old and obsolete by the time they are chosen for a new device. Component chip manufacturers deploy basic, unoptimized versions of Linux, known as Board Support Packages, with minimal engineering modifications. Device Manufacturers then select these cheap chips to build hardware like routers without placing their own brand names on the final box, doing as little engineering as possible before shipping them down the line. Once a product is shipped, no single entity in this fragmented chain has the economic incentive, expertise, or operational capability to develop or distribute patches. Manufacturers find it far more profitable to focus resources on releasing the next version of a product rather than supporting and maintaining the one already on the market. Consequently, software components inside brand-new devices are frequently four to five years older than the device itself, leaving them permanently exposed to well-known vulnerabilities. To create security patches, developers require access to the source code, yet many proprietary device drivers are shipped strictly as compiled binary blobs with no source code available, making third-party patching technically impossible. Relying on users to manually download and install updates is equally ineffective because consumers are rarely alerted to new firmware versions and lack the technical competence to administer these devices.

## Task 2: Critical Commentary and Technical Discussion

The fundamental problem is a market failure: manufacturers have no economic incentive to prioritize secure design, since the financial costs of vulnerabilities fall almost entirely on end users. 

- The technical risk of “bricking” constitutes a serious form of self-inflicted Denial of Service. Embedded systems operate under strict hardware constraints and in diverse environmental configurations, which means that applying an automatic patch without thorough, localized testing can cause a device to be inoperable.
- Operational responsibility and downtime pose equally serious risks, as triggering an uncoordinated automatic update that fails during peak hours of activity can cause devastating downtime for commercial infrastructure.

## Mitigating the Problem: Technological, Market, and Regulatory Solutions
To resolve this crisis, a hybrid approach combining economic pressure, technical standards, and strict regulatory frameworks must be deployed.

> Shifting Market Incentives and ISP Responsibility: Schneier notes that large ISPs are an effective economic lever because they cover the direct support costs of network crashes and compromised user hardware. Following a utility analogy, just as a water utility cuts off service to a house with a severe leak that threatens the neighborhood, ISPs can technically act as network enforcers. If an endpoint device is detected generating malicious botnet traffic, the ISP is the only technical entity capable of isolating the device until the underlying vulnerabilities are remediated.

> From a technical standpoint, regulatory and procurement rules should mandate open-source driver software to eliminate binary blobs. Providing source code allows independent third-party vendors and ISPs to develop, test, and maintain security patches long after the original manufacturer has abandoned the hardware line.

> Regulatory Intervention and Risk Acceptance: regulatory intervention like the EU's **Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)** are necessary to enforce true legal accountability across the digital landscape. The CRA establishes the first uniform cybersecurity rules for products with digital elements. It is specifically designed to correct a market failure where consumers, rather than the manufacturers themselves, end up covering the financial and operational costs of insecure products. To fix these market problems, the CRA requires manufacturers to integrate security directly into the design phase and remain responsible for a product's safety throughout its entire lifecycle. This means companies must define a support period based on expected use (lasting **at least 5 years**) during which they are legally mandated to communicate vulnerabilities and provide security updates. These responsibilities are distributed proportionally across all economic operators in the supply chain, relative to their role. Before any digital product can be sold on the internal market, it must undergo a strict conformity assessment. Depending on the specific risk level of the product, this process involves either a **self-assessment or a third-party validation**. Once compliance is proven, manufacturers draw up an official declaration of conformity and affix the **CE mark**, which allows the product to move freely across the EU. To ensure everyone follows the rules, market surveillance authorities have the power to step in when a product is non-compliant, allowing them to restrict sales, order product recalls, and impose administrative fines.

### Risk Strategy and Legal Interplay

To prevent the spread of unpatched vulnerabilities, the CRA explicitly prefers the risks associated with automatic updates (which may cause temporary device downtime or unexpected software issues) to the danger of leaving millions of devices connected to the Internet permanently unpatched against global threats.

Although the regulation **explicitly excludes Software as a Service (SaaS)**, it complements the **NIS 2 Directive** by ensuring the security of the underlying physical and digital supply chains.

Following the CRA’s formal entry into force, economic operators and Member States are granted a **36-month transition period** to fully adapt to the new requirements. However, since it requires fewer organizational changes, a **21-month deadline** applies specifically to the obligation to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and security incidents.
